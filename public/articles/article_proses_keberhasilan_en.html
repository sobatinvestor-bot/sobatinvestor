Process.

Why every lasting success is built from small, repeated actions — lessons from Chinese, Greek, Arab, Persian, and Japanese traditions, and modern science.

We celebrate outcomes: medals, degrees, IPO valuations, the profit figure on a screen. Outcomes are what's visible, what gets photographed, what gets displayed. But an outcome is only the tip of an iceberg — the small part that surfaces above the waterline. Beneath it, far larger and unseen, is the process: thousands of hours of practice, hundreds of small and tedious decisions, and a patience that no one is watching.

This article is about one simple idea that is difficult to actually live by: lasting success is almost always a consequence, not a goal. It grows out of the right process, repeated, over a long time. We'll look at this through six traditions vastly different in era and place — China, Greece, the Arab-Islamic world, Persia, Japan, and modern science — that, curiously, all point to the same conclusion. They're arranged roughly by chronological order, from oldest to most recent.

The big picture: outcomes are a lagging indicator

Think of an outcome as a lagging indicator of a process. A good outcome can be born from a bad process that got lucky; a bad outcome can be born from a good process that ran into bad luck. This is the most dangerous trap of all: when luck delivers a good result, we mistakenly believe our process was correct, and repeat it until the luck runs out.

That's why judging yourself on a single outcome is misleading. A mature person flips their attention: they invest in the process — the thing they can control — and accept that outcomes arrive as a distribution of possibilities, not a certainty. The same is true in investing: one profitable trade doesn't prove a strategy is correct, and one losing trade doesn't prove it's wrong. What deserves evaluation is the quality of the decision, not a single roll of the dice.

The outcome belongs to time and luck; the process belongs to you.

China: a journey of a thousand li, and a pile of small steps

Classical Chinese thought — Confucianism and Taoism, roughly from the 6th to 3rd century BCE — placed patient self-cultivation above rushed achievement.

From the Dao De Jing, attributed to Laozi, comes a phrase now known worldwide: a journey of a thousand li begins with a single step beneath one's feet. A daunting distance becomes possible not through a leap, but through repeated steps. The philosopher Xunzi (3rd century BCE) put it more sharply still: without accumulating small steps, one will never cover a thousand li — the idea of accumulation (積, jī) that sits at the core of his work ethic.

Confucius, in the Analects, made xiushen (修身, self-cultivation) a foundation: improving oneself bit by bit through relentless learning and practice. And the word gongfu (功夫) — which we now know as "kung fu" — at its root means a skill achieved through hard work and time; it belongs to anyone who devotes themselves to anything long enough. There's also the proverb: dripping water pierces stone (滴水穿石) — not through strength, but through persistence.

Greece: excellence is a habit, not an event

Ancient Greek philosophy placed process at the heart of what it means to live a good life.

For Aristotle, aretē (excellence/virtue) was not a talent one possesses, but something formed through repeated action. The philosopher Will Durant later distilled the core of the Nicomachean Ethics in a widely quoted line: excellence is not an act, but a habit. The purpose of life — eudaimonia, often translated as "flourishing" — was likewise understood as an activity of a virtuous soul, not a state achieved once and possessed forever.

The Stoics added an important distinction. Epictetus, in the Enchiridion, taught the dichotomy of control: some things lie within our power (effort, attitude, discipline — that is, process), and some do not (outcomes, others' judgments, fate). Wisdom is pouring one's full energy into the former, and releasing the latter. Marcus Aurelius wrote notes to himself in the same spirit: do your part, then let the rest unfold.

Arab & Islamic tradition: itqan, ihsan, and effort

The Arab-Islamic tradition (from the 7th century CE, peaking in the Golden Age of the 8th–14th centuries) tied the quality of one's work to spiritual value.

Two words anchor it. Itqan (إتقان) means doing something with care, precision, and thoroughness — quality of work as a virtue, not mere formality. Ihsan (إحسان) means doing one's best, as if the result of one's work were being directly observed. Both shift the focus from merely "finishing" to "doing it right."

There is also a balance between effort and outcome. The teachings of ikhtiar (sincere effort) and tawakkul (trusting reliance after effort) are captured in a well-known piece of advice: tie your camel first, then place your trust [in God]. The human part is sa'y — effort — sustained by sabr (patience); the outcome is entrusted to the Almighty. Ibn Khaldun, in the Muqaddimah (1377), even explained sociologically how skill (malakah, an ingrained habit) forms only through long repetition and practice — well before modern psychology arrived at similar conclusions.

Persia: drop by drop, becoming an ocean

Persian literature and wisdom traditions glorified patience (sabr) and sustained effort (sa'y).

A saying commonly attributed to the poet Saadi of Shiraz (13th century) reads, roughly: drop by drop, a river is formed, then it becomes an ocean. This is one of the most beautiful images of compounding: a single drop looks pointless, but unceasing drops form an ocean. Small, consistent things beat large, occasional ones.

Ferdowsi, the great Persian poet, spent roughly three decades writing the Shahnameh — one of the longest epics in world literature. His famous line asserts that whoever has knowledge has power. But just as important is his life's example: the monumental work itself is proof of process — years of patient labor, long before anyone knew it would become timeless.

Japan: perfecting the small, every day

Japanese work culture gives us several words that resist exact translation, yet capture a devotion to process precisely.

Kaizen (改善) — continuous improvement

Kaizen means "change for the better": improving a little, continuously, rather than waiting for a big leap. Popularized through the Toyota Production System and Masaaki Imai's writing (1986), the idea isn't revolution but the discipline of improving 1% today, then another 1% tomorrow. What looks trivial day to day becomes extraordinary once compounded across years.

Shokunin (職人) — the craftsman's spirit

A shokunin is a craftsperson who devotes their life to mastering their craft — not for recognition, but for the perfection of the work itself. A figure like Jiro Ono, the sushi chef immortalized in the documentary Jiro Dreams of Sushi (2011), spent decades refining the very same motions. For the shokunin, process isn't a path toward a goal; process is the goal.

Shuhari (守破離) — the stages of mastery

A concept from martial arts and traditional crafts that divides mastery into three stages: shu (obey the basic rules), ha (begin to deviate and experiment), ri (transcend the rules to find one's own way). The message is clear: no one becomes an expert instantly. Mastery is a sequence of stages to be passed through, not a door to be forced open.

Modern science: systems beat goals

20th- and 21st-century science and research tested these old intuitions — and largely supported them.

  • Deliberate practice. Research by Anders Ericsson and colleagues (1993) showed that elite-level skill is determined more by focused, feedback-rich practice than by hours logged or raw talent alone. It's not the sheer number of repetitions, but the quality of the process. A fair caveat: its role is debated — a meta-analysis by Macnamara, Hambrick & Oswald (2014) found deliberate practice explains some, not all, of the variance in achievement (a large share in games and music, a small share in some professions); talent, starting age, and other factors still matter. Process is necessary — it's just not the only determinant.
  • Systems over goals. James Clear, in Atomic Habits (2018), argues that we don't rise to the level of our goals, we fall to the level of our systems. Goals set the direction; systems (daily processes) determine the progress.
  • Growth mindset. Carol Dweck (2006) distinguished between a fixed and a growth mindset: believing that ability can grow through effort makes people more resilient to failure — because failure is read as part of the process, not a verdict.
  • Grit. Angela Duckworth (2016) found that long-term perseverance toward a single goal — a combination of passion and persistence — often explains achievement better than raw initial talent.

It all comes down to the simple mathematics of accumulation: a 1% improvement every day, sustained over a year, compounds ability far more than occasional bursts of great effort. Compound interest isn't just a law of finance; it's a law of skill, habit, and reputation.

The common thread: six traditions, one truth

Notice how far apart these are — a school of thought in ancient China, the agora in Athens, the halls of learning in Baghdad, a poet's garden in Shiraz, a Zen monastery in Japan, and a modern psychology laboratory — yet all six point in the same direction. Laozi's "thousand-li step" and Xunzi's "accumulation" are older siblings of Saadi's compounding and modern deliberate practice. Japanese kaizen and Arab itqan both worship a quality of work pursued relentlessly. Shokunin and aretē both see excellence as a habit, not an event. And the Stoic dichotomy of control blends with tawakkul-after-ikhtiar: pour your energy into the process, then release the outcome.

This cross-era agreement is not a coincidence. It tells us something stable about the world: outcomes cannot be commanded, but process can be lived. So the only sensible strategy is to fall in love with the process.

What this means in practice

Turning this belief into action isn't complicated, but it demands honesty:

  1. Define the process, not just the target. "Getting rich" is an outcome; "saving and reviewing my portfolio every month" is a process. You can only actually carry out the latter.
  2. Measure inputs, not just outputs. Track hours of practice, consistency of habits, quality of decisions — not merely a final number that's partly determined by luck.
  3. Improve a little, continuously. Find one small thing to improve today. Kaizen beats waiting for a big inspiration that rarely comes.
  4. Separate the decision from the outcome. Judge whether the process was right, regardless of whether this particular attempt won or lost. A good decision can turn out badly; a bad decision can turn out well. Don't confuse the two.
  5. Be patient with the timeline. Accumulation takes time to become visible. The quietest part of the process — before there's any result — is exactly the part that matters most.

Closing

Success without process is luck in disguise — beautiful for a moment, then it evaporates because it can't be repeated. Success built on process doesn't guarantee a win every time, but it makes you deserve to win over time, and gives you a calm that doesn't depend on today's result.

This is the philosophy we hold at Sobat Investor: valuing transparent methods, disciplined habits, and uncertainty stated as it is — not the promise of certainty. In investing as in life, you do not control the outcome. But you always hold the process. Start there, drop by drop.
